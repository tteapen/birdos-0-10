# BIRDOS

Bioinspired Research Design for Organization Science.

BIRDOS is a protocol for generating candidate theoretical propositions by transferring causal mechanisms from biology to organizational settings, and for keeping a record of the pathways that were rejected along the way.

## 1. What the tool does

A researcher states an organizational problem. The tool restates it as a theoretical problem, offers a set of biological phenomena whose causal structure may transfer, and then works through six stages: it structures the phenomenon, extracts alternative causal accounts, strips the biology out of them, asks what would have to be true in an organization for each account to hold, applies five evaluation criteria in a fixed order, and assembles what survives together with a record of what did not.

Two decisions belong to the researcher and are never made by the tool. The first is which theoretical framing of the problem to adopt. The second is which phenomenon to work with. Inside the six stages the researcher also decides which causal accounts proceed, can revise an abstraction before it is mapped, and can disagree with any evaluation. Each of these interventions is recorded alongside the output, so a later reader can separate the researcher's judgment from the tool's suggestions.

The output is a set of candidate propositions with stated boundary conditions and falsification tests, and a corpus of rejected pathways with reasons. Neither output is a finding. Both are inputs to the work a researcher would do next.

## 2. The problem it addresses

Organization theory has borrowed from biology for well over a century, and the borrowing has a difficult history. The nineteenth century organismic tradition treated society as a body with organs and functions, which produced description rather than explanation. The modern debate begins with Alchian (1950), who argued that competitive selection could explain the survival of profitable firms without assuming that managers optimize.

Penrose (1952) answered him, and her objection remains the strongest one any bioinspired proposal has to meet. She argued that biological analogies in economics were used to avoid explanation rather than to supply it. Selection language, she wrote, describes an outcome without identifying any process that produces it in firms, and it does this by quietly removing human purpose from the account. Firms are run by people who form expectations and choose, and an analogy that replaces those choices with an automatic mechanism has not explained the firm. Penrose also noted that analogies are rarely tested. Once adopted they are extended, and the extension is accepted because the original comparison felt apt.

The two large research programs that followed drew the same criticism in different forms. Population ecology (Hannan and Freeman, 1977) imported selection at the level of organizational populations. Evolutionary theories of the firm (Nelson and Winter, 1982) treated routines as the unit of inheritance. Both were productive, and both were charged with taking the vocabulary of Darwinian selection without taking its mechanism. Hodgson and Knudsen (2010) tried to supply the missing mechanism directly. Reydon and Scholz (2015) argued that the attempt did not succeed, because organizational populations do not have the properties that make selection explanatory in biology.

The failure in each case has a common shape. A term that carries a specific causal process in biology is applied to organizations where that process is absent, and the term keeps its explanatory force by association. The response BIRDOS takes is not to argue that biology is a better source than critics allowed. It is to make the step where the failure happens into an explicit, recorded stage that can be inspected.

One part of Penrose's objection is conceded rather than answered. The tool can require that a transferred mechanism be stated as a causal structure with conditions that could fail. It cannot establish that the mechanism operates through the decisions of people in roles rather than around them. That question belongs to the empirical work a researcher does after the tool has finished, and no procedural safeguard settles it in advance.

## 3. Why unfamiliar phenomena

Gentner (1983) distinguishes surface similarity from structural similarity. Two domains can share attributes while sharing no relations, and they can share relations while sharing no surface features. Productive analogies are the second kind. The distinction is well established, and the practical difficulty is that it is easy to state and hard to apply, because a comparison that shares only surface features often feels structural to the person making it.

This is where familiarity does damage. When a reader is told that firms compete like species, the reader supplies the missing structure from memory. The analogy is never forced to state a causal claim that could be wrong, and it reads as illuminating for that reason. Sources that organization theory has already absorbed are the ones most exposed to this, because the absorption is what makes the completion automatic.

Sources the field has not absorbed cannot be completed this way. If a researcher is told that a fungal network thickens the paths it uses and dismantles the ones it does not, no stored organizational association is available, so the mechanism has to be written out before anything can be done with it. The constraint, rather than any particular biological insight, is what the library of unfamiliar phenomena is for.

Three lines of work in organization theory point the same way. Tsoukas (1991) argued that a metaphor becomes scientifically useful when it is transformed into a statement about mechanisms. Cornelissen (2005) treated metaphor as an interaction between domains that generates new structure rather than transferring existing structure. Oswick, Keenoy and Grant (2002) argued that dissimilarity between domains does more theoretical work than similarity, because a comparison that fails at a specific point tells the researcher which property the target lacks. BIRDOS treats that last point as an output rather than a residual, and every candidate proposition carries a statement of where the biological mechanism stops applying.

The wider frame is Weick (1989) on theory construction as disciplined imagination. In that account theorizing is a selection process applied to conjectures, and its quality depends on the number of independent conjectures generated and the strength of the criteria applied to them. Generative tools change the cost of the first. The value of the workflow rests on the second, which is why the evaluation criteria are fixed in advance and applied in a stated order.

## 4. The framework

The workflow has three setup steps, a point at which the problem and the phenomenon are fixed, and six stages. Any stage can be re-run without starting again, and re-running a stage clears the stages after it, because a revised abstraction invalidates the propositions built on it.

The pivot of the whole workflow is the second setup step, which restates the researcher's problem as a structural signature. A structural signature describes what the system is doing without reference to organizations. Two examples from the worked cases give the form. A system holds a reserve whose condition cannot be observed without using it, and using it consumes part of what is being checked. A system generates candidates at near zero cost while the stage that evaluates them has fixed capacity, so throughput is set downstream.

The signature, not the researcher's original wording, is what the phenomenon library is searched against. This matters for the quality of the search. A question phrased in organizational vocabulary retrieves phenomena that share that vocabulary, which is the surface matching Gentner warns against. A question phrased as a structural signature retrieves phenomena that share a causal shape.

A useful signature is one that could be false. If the restatement describes a situation without making a claim about it, the later stages have nothing to test and the propositions they produce will be vague in the same way. The researcher chooses among three restatements and can ask for another set, and the number of rounds is recorded.

## 5. The six modules

| Module | What it does |
|---|---|
| 1 intake | Converts a description of the phenomenon into structured fields covering actors, resources, triggers, timing, costs, outcomes and constraints. Fields the source does not support are marked unknown. |
| 2 mechanism | Produces several candidate causal accounts of how the phenomenon produces its outcome, and identifies accounts that are near duplicates of one another. |
| 3 abstraction | Restates each account without biological terminology and judges whether a meaningful causal claim survives the removal. |
| 4 mapping | Asks what would have to be true in an organization for the abstracted mechanism to operate, and states settings, antecedents, outcomes and boundary conditions. |
| 5 filtering | Applies five criteria in a fixed order, records the reason a pathway is not carried forward, and gives a preliminary assessment of overlap with existing organizational research. |
| 6 output | Assembles surviving propositions with their derivation, boundary conditions and falsification tests, and retains the rejected pathways. |

The intake stage carries more weight than its position suggests. Every later stage treats its fields as given, so a detail supplied by assumption rather than by the source will pass through mechanism extraction, survive abstraction, produce a confident proposition and clear all five criteria without anything re-examining it. Marking a field unknown is the correct result whenever the alternative is a guess, and a researcher reading the output should read the unknowns first.

Mechanism extraction produces alternatives rather than one account, and flags those that restate one another. The count of genuinely distinct accounts is informative in itself. A phenomenon that supports one mechanism dressed four ways is a weaker source than one that supports three, and it is better to learn this before investing in it. The definition of a mechanism used here follows Machamer, Darden and Craver (2000): a mechanism names entities and the activities by which they produce a regular outcome. A statement that something is efficient or adaptive is not a mechanism.

Abstraction is where the distinction between surface and structural similarity becomes a test rather than a principle. Each account is restated with the biology removed and judged on whether a claim that could be false survives. Statements that become vacuous, such as the observation that a system responds to its environment, are marked as thin.

Mapping asks what conditions an organization would have to satisfy for the abstracted mechanism to operate, and reports whether organizational actors and processes can satisfy them. It also states where the mechanism stops applying. That statement is frequently the more useful output. A mapping that fails at an identifiable point names the property organizations lack, which is a sharper question than the proposition it replaces.

## 6. Filtering and the rejection record

| Criterion | The question it asks | What a rejection here indicates |
|---|---|---|
| abstraction | Is the mapping mechanistic rather than verbal? | A word is carrying the claim. The abstraction was usually thin and should have been caught earlier. |
| feasibility | Could a researcher observe these constructs? | The mechanism may hold but the constructs are not measurable as stated. |
| novelty | Does the claim depart from what the field already holds? | The workflow has rediscovered a known result. This is a finding about the source, not a fault. |
| falsifiability | Is there an observation that would count against it? | Often a claim about what is optimal with no criterion attached. Frequently recoverable. |
| usefulness | Would the answer change how scholars theorize? | True, testable and inconsequential. |

Five criteria are applied in a fixed order, and a candidate is rejected by the first one it fails. Later criteria are not applied to a rejected candidate, so each rejection has a single named cause rather than a composite score. The criteria draw on Bacharach (1989) on falsifiability and utility as the tests a theory must meet, and on Corley and Gioia (2011) on originality and utility as the components of a theoretical contribution.

A run in which nothing is rejected is a failed run. If the criteria never exclude anything they are decoration, and the record they produce has no value.

Rejections are recorded at two points in the workflow: phenomena the suggestion step considered and set aside, and candidates a criterion excluded. Each entry keeps the reason, the stage at which it was excluded, and any disagreement the researcher registered. A researcher who thinks a rejection is wrong records why, and that reason is stored beside the original rather than replacing it.

Organization science has no systematic record of the theory it decided not to build. Published papers report what survived, and discarded pathways exist only in the memory of whoever discarded them. This is the file drawer problem applied to theorizing rather than to results. Keeping the record makes theory generation itself available for study: which pathways recur across problems, which fail at the same criterion, and whether particular sources reliably produce claims that are testable but of no consequence. For doctoral researchers the record is often more useful than the propositions, because it shows what a dead end looks like before a year has been spent in one.

## 7. Using the tool

State the problem as you would state it to a colleague. A causal situation carries further than a topic. The phrase "organizational capabilities" gives the workflow nothing to work with. The observation that capabilities persist as commitments after the demand that justified them has gone gives it a situation with parts that interact.

When choosing among the three restatements, apply two tests. Ask which puzzle you actually want to work on, since the choice determines everything downstream. Then ask which statement could be false. A restatement that nothing could contradict will produce propositions with the same property.

When choosing a phenomenon, prefer sources the field has not already absorbed. This is counterintuitive, because the familiar suggestion usually reads as the most apt, and its aptness is exactly the property that lets a reader complete the mechanism without your having specified it. If a phenomenon indexed under a different theme appears in the suggestions, examine it closely. The index records a resemblance and the workflow needs a mechanism, so the two do not always coincide. The suggestions narrow the field, and the choice is the researcher's in every case.

Read the output in a particular order. Start with the fields marked unknown at intake, since an invented detail there will not be caught later. Then read the accounts marked as near duplicates, which tell you how much real variety the phenomenon supports. Then read the thin verdicts at abstraction. Then read the statements of where each mapping breaks, including on candidates that were rejected, because a mapping that fails at an identifiable point often carries more than one that survives.

Editing an abstraction is a normal part of the work rather than an intervention of last resort. Abstractions are frequently almost right, and tightening one clause turns a vague statement into a testable one. Edits are recorded, so the provenance of any proposition built on an edited abstraction remains clear.

## 8. Reporting a run

Each run produces a record containing the problem as it was typed, the restatement that was chosen and how many rounds it took, the phenomenon and whether it came from the suggestions or from the library directly, the structured intake with its status markings, the accounts and abstractions carried forward, the researcher's edits, the evaluation of each candidate, and the rejected pathways. The record is downloadable and should be kept with any proposition taken forward.

A methods paragraph reporting BIRDOS-assisted theorizing should state the problem as it was originally posed, the structural signature adopted, the phenomenon used and how it was selected, the number of candidates generated and rejected, the criterion each rejection failed, and the points at which the researcher intervened. A reader who has that paragraph and the run record can reconstruct how the proposition was arrived at and disagree with any step.

Reproduction here means re-running the recorded settings and obtaining results a reader would recognise as the same, rather than obtaining identical wording. Generated text varies between runs even under fixed settings. The claim the record supports is about the derivation being inspectable, not about the output being deterministic.

## 9. Evaluating whether it works

The relevant question is not whether the tool produces plausible propositions. Plausible propositions are easy to produce and prove nothing. Three tests are more informative.

The first is blind expert evaluation. Organizational scholars rate propositions for novelty and usefulness without knowing which were produced by the workflow and which by researchers working conventionally on the same problems. What matters is whether the ratings differ and whether raters agree with one another.

The second is an audit of the biological claims by domain experts, reported as an error rate rather than as an assurance. Claims about source phenomena are the output most exposed to factual error, and an audited error rate is the honest way to state how much weight the biology can carry.

The third is the one that matters most and is the least advanced. A proposition generated through the workflow has to be taken through empirical testing. Until that has been done at least once, the case for the approach rests on the quality of the procedure rather than on the validity of anything it has produced, and a reviewer is entitled to weigh it accordingly.

## 10. Scope and accountability

The workflow does not establish that a proposition is true. Passing five criteria is a screen against common defects, not evidence. It does not verify the biology, which is why source claims are flagged for expert checking. It does not conduct a literature review; the overlap assessment at the filtering stage is a prompt for the researcher to check a specific idea, and it can be wrong. It does not choose the research question or the phenomenon.

Responsibility for any claim that leaves the workflow rests with the researcher who publishes it. The run record exists so that the reasoning can be inspected by others, which is a support for accountability rather than a substitute for it.

Text entered into the tool is sent to a third-party model provider for processing. Nothing is stored on a server by the tool itself, and the session record is held in the browser until the page is reloaded.

Three problems worked from question to proposition are available separately, with the phenomena considered, the accounts carried forward, the candidates rejected and the reasons in each case.
